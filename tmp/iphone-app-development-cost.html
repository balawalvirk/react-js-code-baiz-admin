1. The Short Answer: What an iPhone App Actually Costs

Start with the bands, because the bands are what you budget against. These are the ranges we publish on our cost pages, and they apply to an iPhone build exactly as they apply to any other platform.

The published build bands:

  • Validation MVP: $10,000 to $20,000. The smallest version that can win real users. One core job, done properly, on foundations that do not need a rebuild if it works.
  • Simpler single platform app: from $25,000. A few screens, basic accounts, no custom backend.
  • Production grade app: $60,000 to $120,000 or more. A custom backend, payments, an admin panel, proper testing and the store launch handled. The band most real businesses land in.
  • Maintenance: 15 to 20 percent of the build cost per year. Operating system updates, store policy changes and small fixes.

Our full app development cost breakdown gathers every figure we publish in one place, and our long cost guide explains the drivers behind each band. The question is which band honestly matches your scope. The $18,000 quote in the opening story was not a bargain version of the $95,000 quote. It was a smaller promise wearing the same label, with the backend, the testing and the launch quietly removed.

When two quotes for the same app differ by five times, they are rarely quotes for the same app. Ask each agency what is excluded, in writing, before you compare the numbers.

2. Why There Is No Separate iPhone Price

Founders search for ios app development cost expecting Apple to add a premium somewhere. It does not work that way.

The cost of an app is driven by scope, not by the logo on the phone. Accounts, payments, a backend, an admin panel and the number of screens and flows consume the design, build and testing time, on any device. That is why our iOS app development page publishes the general ranges rather than an iPhone only table. The platform is not the expensive part. The product is.

What this means for you as a buyer:

  • Treat an iPhone premium as a red flag. If an agency charges extra simply because the app is for iPhone, ask them to name the extra work. Usually there is none to name.
  • Treat a per screen price the same way. A login screen and a payment flow with refunds and receipts are both one screen on a feature list and very different amounts of work.
  • Compare scope, not platforms. Two written scopes covering the same features, backend and launch support can be compared honestly. Headline numbers cannot.

The real iPhone specific costs are smaller than a premium: the Apple fee, payments set up the way Apple requires, and review time. Sections 4 and 7 cover each at its real size.

3. What Drives the iPhone Number Up or Down

Within a band, scope decides where you land. These drivers move an iPhone quote most, in the order they usually move it.

The drivers that matter most:

  • Number of features in version one. The single biggest lever on any quote. Every extra flow is design, build, testing and maintenance, forever.
  • Backend and admin. An app that stores accounts, content or orders needs a server side, and the business usually needs an admin panel. A custom backend is the main step between the simpler band and the production grade band.
  • Payments and subscriptions. Taking money the way Apple requires, including in-app purchases and subscriptions set up correctly, plus Sign in with Apple, is real work, and the work App Store review examines most closely.
  • Integrations. Maps, chat, third party services and data feeds each add build time and a dependency that can misbehave.
  • Design polish. Interfaces that follow Apple conventions, so the app feels at home on an iPhone from the first screen, take proper design time.

Notice what is not on that list: the iPhone itself. To lower a quote, do not negotiate the platform. Cut the feature list. Our rule for version one: one problem, one core feature set, one platform decision made honestly. Everything else goes on a named list for version two.

4. The Small Costs Founders Forget to Budget

The build quote is not the whole first year cost. Three smaller items belong in every iPhone budget from the start.

Budget for these alongside the build:

  • Apple Developer Program: $99 per year. Apple's own fee for the account your app is published under, verified on Apple's developer site. It matters out of proportion to its size, because the account must be in your name, not your developer's. Your app, your store account, your data, from day one.
  • Maintenance: 15 to 20 percent of build cost per year. Every iOS release changes something. Store policies change. Dependencies age. Maintenance is the planned version of that work, instead of emergency work at the worst moment.
  • Launch fixes reserve. Every first launch finds issues no test caught. Hold a small reserve so a normal week one fix is a task, not a crisis. The amount is your decision; the mistake is budgeting zero.

Together, these items are the difference between a budget that survives reality and one that only works if nothing unexpected happens.

5. The Timeline: How Long an iPhone App Takes, Stage by Stage

Cost is only half the question. The other half is how long does an ios app take to reach the App Store, and the answer follows the same bands as the money.

The published timeline bands:

  • Simple app: 2 to 4 months. A focused first version, a handful of screens, one main job done well.
  • Typical app: 4 to 6 months. Accounts, a backend, payments, notifications and an admin panel. This is what most founders actually build.
  • Complex app: 6 to 9 months or more. Marketplaces, social features, real time data, or anything handling a lot of users doing a lot of things at once.

Our timeline guide breaks a typical build into the phases behind those bands: discovery and scoping first, then design ending in a clickable prototype you approve, then development, usually about half the total timeline, run in sprints with a working version on your own phone. Testing overlaps the build, and launch and store review close the project.

What actually slows an iPhone project down:

  • Scope creep during the build. Features added mid build move the finish line every time.
  • Slow decisions. A question asked on Monday and answered on Friday, repeated across a project, costs more calendar time than any coding problem.
  • Design changes after development starts. Changing a screen in a design file takes minutes. In built code it takes days.
  • Review rounds. A rejection that needs a code change adds days each time, which is why section 7 budgets for it.

The fastest way to ship is to cut scope, not quality. A small, excellent app in three months beats a big, mediocre one in nine, every time.

6. Device Testing on iPhone: Narrower, Not Free

One place the iPhone genuinely differs is testing, in the founder's favour. An iPhone app needs to work well on a narrow range of current iPhones: a short, known list of devices and screen sizes that an honest team can test thoroughly.

That does not make testing optional. We test on real devices, not just simulators, because simulators do not reproduce how an app behaves on a real phone on a real connection. Camera behaviour, notifications, payments and performance on a weak signal only show up on hardware.

If you later add a second platform, testing is one of the costs that grows, because the other platform brings a far wider range of devices. Our guide to iOS vs Android, which to build first runs that comparison in full.

7. App Store Review: The Part of the Timeline You Do Not Control

Every iPhone timeline ends with a stage your team does not control. Apple reviews every app before it goes live, checking policy compliance, privacy details, payments and basic quality. Plan for it and it is a short stage. Pretend it is instant and it will move your launch date for you.

The planning facts:

  • Review usually takes 24 to 72 hours. The typical window for a compliant submission, not a promise. Review times are Apple's, not ours.
  • Budget one to two weeks for the launch stage. That covers the review, any questions Apple raises, and a rejection round with a fix and resubmission if needed. Each round adds a few days.
  • Most first submission problems are preventable. Accounts, payments and privacy details set up correctly, a working app with no placeholder content, and honest store metadata are what a clean first pass is made of.
  • TestFlight comes before the public launch. Beta builds go to real testers through TestFlight first, so the version submitted publicly is one real people have already used.

Never promise customers a launch date that assumes a first time pass through App Store review. Promise the submission date, which you control, and give the launch as the window the review budget produces.

A team that has handled submission across hundreds of launches cannot make Apple faster. It can submit an app with nothing obvious to reject and answer review questions the same day.

8. What Happens to the Cost If Android Follows

Many founders who start with an iPhone plan to add Android later, and the right time to think about that cost is before the first line of code, because the build route you choose now decides it.

There are two honest routes. Built natively for iOS only, the Android version later is a second build, priced as a new app layer on the shared backend and product thinking. Built from the start from one cross-platform codebase, the way most of our clients launch, one codebase costs less to build and to maintain than two separate native builds. And a second platform added later almost always costs more than building it in from the start.

So the decision is really this:

  • iPhone only, genuinely. Your early users are on iPhone, budget is tight, and there is no dated plan for Android. Build iPhone well.
  • Both audiences at launch. Your waitlist or customers are split. Price the one codebase route now, before a native build makes the second platform a second project.
  • iPhone now, Android soon and dated. Treat the follow on as part of this decision. Get both routes priced in writing and compare totals across the whole plan.

None of this changes the iPhone bands in section 1. It changes what the total programme costs across both platforms.

Then run the Android Follow-On Test before you commit:

  1. Is a second platform dated, or just hoped for? A date inside the next year argues for pricing the one codebase route now. No date argues for a clean single platform build.
  2. What share of your named early users are not on iPhone? Named users, from your waitlist or customers, not national statistics. A large named share changes the maths more than any chart.

The planner does not give you a quote. It gives you a defensible budget range and a launch window you can plan a business around.

10. How to Get an Exact iPhone Number

A range becomes a number through scope, and only through scope. Any exact figure offered before a team understands your app is theatre.

The route from range to written quote:

  • Fit check first. A short conversation about the product, the users and what version one really needs. If a team is not the right fit, a good one says so here.
  • Scope in writing. Features listed and agreed, with the things left for later named as clearly as the things included. This is what makes quotes comparable.
  • Both platform options priced. If Android is anywhere in your future, the quote should price the iPhone only route and the one codebase route side by side, so the follow on cost in section 8 is a number, not a surprise.
  • Cost and timeline together. A quote with a price and no timeline, or a timeline and no review budget, is half a quote. You need both in writing before money moves.

Hold every agency you talk to, including us, to that standard. It was never really about the phone. It is about whether the scope, the testing and the launch are inside the number you were given.
