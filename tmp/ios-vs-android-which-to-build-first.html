A founder in Brooklyn asked us this last month with a fixed budget and a launch date already promised to early customers. Half her waitlist used iPhones. Half used Android phones. She could afford to build one platform well, or both platforms thinly, and she wanted a straight answer to a simple question: iOS vs Android, which to build first?

There is no universal winner. There is a right answer for your audience, your budget and your revenue plan, and it is usually visible once you compare the two platforms on the four things that actually decide a first launch.

TL;DR: Build first where your paying users already are. In the United States that is often iOS, because iPhone users tend to spend more in apps and US iPhone share is high. Build Android first if your audience is international, price sensitive, or tied to Android hardware. If you need both audiences at launch and your app has no heavy native features, build once with cross-platform and skip the choice entirely.

The decision at a glance:

  1. Audience first. Where do your actual waitlist, customers and traffic live today?
  2. Revenue second. Which group is more likely to pay, and how much?
  3. Budget third. One platform built well beats two platforms built thinly.
  4. Speed fourth. Review times, device testing and release control differ.
  5. Cross-platform check. If both audiences matter now, one shared codebase may be the honest answer.

1. Start With Your Audience, Not the Market Share Chart

Global market share is the most quoted and least useful number in this decision. Worldwide, Android holds the larger share. In the United States, iPhone holds a much stronger position, especially among higher income buyers. Neither chart tells you what your users carry in their pockets.

Ask these questions before you look at any chart:

  • What does your waitlist use? Add a simple device question to your signup form, or check the mobile split in your website analytics. Real signups beat national averages every time.
  • What do your best customers use? If twenty paying customers all use iPhones, that is your launch audience, whatever the global numbers say.
  • Where will your first users come from? A local US service business, a global productivity tool and a price sensitive overseas market are three different answers.
  • Does your app depend on the phone itself? Hardware integrations, background behaviour and some system features still behave differently on each platform.

Your first platform is not a technology bet. It is a customer bet. Build first for the people most likely to pay you in the next ninety days.

2. The Side by Side Comparison Founders Actually Need

This is the comparison we run with founders, stripped of developer preferences. Read it as a decision table, not a contest.

  • Audience: iOS first: Strongest in the US, especially higher income users; Android first: Strongest worldwide, especially outside the US and Europe
  • Spending: iOS first: iPhone users historically spend more on apps and in app purchases; Android first: Larger total user base, lower average spend per user
  • Devices to test: iOS first: A narrow range of current iPhones; Android first: Hundreds of makers, screen sizes and Android versions
  • Store review: iOS first: Stricter review, often slower and less predictable; Android first: Usually faster review, with clearer automated checks
  • Release control: iOS first: Every update waits for store review unless you use approved tools; Android first: Faster rollout controls and staged releases
  • Build cost, single platform: iOS first: Similar core cost, lower testing cost; Android first: Similar core cost, higher testing cost
  • Best first fit: iOS first: Paid consumer apps, US audiences, premium services; Android first: Global audiences, utility apps, hardware linked products

Two patterns matter most in that table. First, testing cost is not equal. A small set of current iPhones is quick to test well. Android fragmentation means more devices, more screen sizes and more edge cases, and honest teams budget time for it. Second, review risk is not equal. Apple review is stricter, and a first submission can come back with questions that add days or weeks. Neither point should decide the launch alone, but both belong in your timeline and your budget.

3. Budget: One Platform Built Well Beats Two Built Thinly

Founders rarely ask should I build iOS or Android first because they prefer one system. They ask because the money only stretches so far. So be blunt about the maths.

Building one native platform first gives you a complete app, a real launch and real user data, usually for the lowest total risk. Building two native apps at once roughly doubles the app build and doubles much of the testing, design adaptation and maintenance that follows. The backend, brand and product thinking are shared, so it is not exactly double the whole project, but the app layer itself is close.

Where a single platform budget goes furthest:

  • A complete core flow. Signup, the main job the app does, payment if you charge, and support basics, all finished properly.
  • Proper testing on real devices. Not just the newest phone in the office.
  • A small reserve for launch fixes. Every first launch finds issues. A budget with no reserve turns a normal fix into a crisis.
  • Analytics from day one. You need to know what first users do, so the second platform decision is based on evidence.

Where a split budget usually hurts:

  • Both apps launch with missing edge cases. Login failures, payment errors and notification problems hit trust hardest in week one.
  • Design gets copied, not adapted. iPhone and Android users expect different navigation habits. A rushed copy feels wrong on both.
  • There is nothing left for iteration. The most valuable build is the one right after launch, when real behaviour replaces guesses.

4. Revenue: Follow the Money Your Model Needs

Different business models point to different first platforms.

Build iOS first when:

  • You charge a subscription or a premium price. iPhone users have a long track record of paying for apps and renewing subscriptions at higher rates.
  • Your buyers are US professionals or higher income consumers. That audience skews strongly to iPhone.
  • Your price point needs trust and polish. A premium service sold to careful buyers benefits from launching where those buyers already spend.

Build Android first when:

  • Your market is global or price sensitive. In many countries Android is not the cheaper option, it is simply the market.
  • Your model is advertising or volume based. Total reach matters more than spend per user.
  • Your users rely on Android only features or hardware. Field tools, scanners and some business devices live in the Android world.

Notice what is not on either list: what your developer personally prefers, and what a competitor launched three years ago. Revenue logic is about your model, your price and your buyers today.

5. Speed: Review Times, Testing and Release Control

If you have a fixed launch date, a funding milestone, a seasonal window or a promised customer start, speed differences stop being trivia.

The speed factors that actually move a launch date:

  • Apple review on a first submission. Plan for questions, especially around payments, privacy and account deletion. A clean, well prepared first submission can pass quickly, but you cannot promise a date around it.
  • Google Play review. Usually faster and more automated, though new developer accounts face extra testing requirements before production access.
  • Device testing time. iOS testing is a short, known list. Android testing needs a deliberate device matrix, and skipping it is how launch day bugs happen.
  • Update speed after launch. Both stores control native releases. Planning your update approach early, including how urgent fixes ship, saves panic later.

Our iOS app development service and Android app development service pages each cover their store process in more detail. The planning rule is simple: whichever platform you choose first, submit early, expect one round of questions, and never sell a launch date that assumes a first time pass.

6. When Cross-Platform Removes the Choice Entirely

For a large share of founder apps, the honest answer to iPhone or Android app first is neither. Build once, launch on both.

Cross-platform frameworks, mainly React Native and Flutter, let one codebase serve both stores. We build in React Native, and we wrote a full comparison of React Native vs native development for founders weighing that route. Here is the decision in short form.

Cross-platform usually wins when:

  • Both audiences matter at launch. A marketplace, a community app or a service with mixed device users cannot afford to exclude half its market.
  • The app is a standard product shape. Accounts, content, booking, commerce, messaging, maps and payments are all well proven ground.
  • Speed and budget both matter. One team, one codebase and one testing programme, with platform specific polish where it counts.
  • You plan to iterate fast after launch. One fix ships to everyone, instead of two builds drifting apart.

Choose native, one platform first, when:

  • The app leans hard on one system. Heavy background processing, deep hardware access, advanced graphics or very specific platform features can justify a native build.
  • Performance margins are extreme. Most apps never hit these limits, but some media, gaming and real time products do.
  • Only one audience exists. An internal tool for company issued iPhones does not need Android on day one, and the reverse is equally true.

The platform question has changed. Ten years ago it meant picking a side. Today, for most founder apps, it means deciding whether there is a real technical reason not to serve both audiences from one codebase.

7. A Simple Decision You Can Run Today

If you are still torn, score your own situation honestly. No scoring system replaces judgment, but this one stops the loudest opinion in the room from deciding.

  1. List your first one hundred likely users. From your waitlist, customers, community or local market, not from a guess.
  2. Mark the device each group mainly uses. Use analytics, a signup question or direct answers.
  3. Mark how each group pays. Subscription, one off purchase, free with ads, or paid by a business.
  4. Price both routes properly. One native platform, two native platforms, and one cross-platform build, each including testing and launch support.
  5. Check for hard technical constraints. List any feature that truly needs native access, and verify it, do not assume it.
  6. Decide, then commit for the launch. Platform regret after launch is normal. Changing direction mid build is what actually burns money.

Most founders who run this find the answer was sitting in step two all along. The device split of real, named, likely buyers settles arguments that market share charts never will.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I build iOS or Android first if I can only afford one? Build where your paying users are. For many US consumer apps that is iOS, because iPhone users spend more and US iPhone share is high. For global, price sensitive or hardware linked audiences, Android first is often the smarter single platform launch.

Is it cheaper to build an iPhone app or an Android app? The core build cost is similar. Android usually costs more to test properly because there are far more devices and screen sizes in use. iOS can cost more time at the review stage. A cross-platform build often costs less than the two native builds combined.

What percentage of users are on iPhone vs Android? Android holds the larger global share, while iPhone holds a much stronger share in the United States, especially among higher income users. Your own audience split matters more than either figure, so check your waitlist and website analytics before deciding.

Can I add the second platform later? Yes, and many successful apps do exactly that. Launch well on one platform, learn from real users, then expand. If you built natively, the second app is a new build informed by real data. If you built cross-platform, both platforms were there from the start.

Does Apple reject more apps than Google? Apple review is generally stricter and less predictable, especially on payments, privacy and user generated content. Google Play review is usually faster, but new accounts face added testing requirements. Either way, an experienced team prepares the submission to reduce avoidable delays.
