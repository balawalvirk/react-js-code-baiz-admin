Picture a founder whose app worked perfectly on their own phone. Then real customers opened it on older, cheaper Android phones, and the layout broke, the camera flow stalled, and the one star reviews started. The app had only ever been tested on one device, in an ecosystem built on thousands. That gap, between one phone and the real Android world, is where most confusion about android app development cost actually lives.

1. The Short Answer: What an Android App Actually Costs

Start with the bands, because the bands are what you budget against. These are the ranges we publish on our cost pages, and they apply to an Android build exactly as they apply to any other platform.

The published build bands:

  • Validation MVP: $10,000 to $20,000. The smallest version that can win real users. One core job, done properly, on foundations that do not need a rebuild if it works.
  • Simpler single platform app: from $25,000. A few screens, basic accounts, no custom backend.
  • Production grade app: $60,000 to $120,000 or more. A custom backend, payments, an admin panel, proper testing across real devices and the Play launch handled. The band most real businesses land in.
  • Maintenance: 15 to 20 percent of the build cost per year. Android version updates, Play policy changes and small fixes.

Our Android app development page publishes these ranges with the testing point stated plainly, our full app development cost breakdown gathers every figure we publish in one place, and our long cost guide explains the drivers behind each band. The companion question is what the other platform costs, and our iPhone app development cost guide answers it with the same bands, because the bands are driven by scope, not by the logo on the phone.

When an Android quote looks unusually cheap, ask what testing was removed to get there. An app tested on one phone is not a cheaper version of the same app. It is an unfinished one.

2. Why There Is No Separate Android Price

Founders search for how much does an android app cost expecting either a discount or a premium. Neither exists in our published ranges. The expensive parts of an app are the same on every platform: accounts, payments, a backend, an admin panel and the number of screens and flows. Android changes the shape of one part of that work, the testing, and the store process at the end.

What this means for you as a buyer:

  • Treat an Android discount as a question, not a win. If an agency charges much less simply because the app is for Android, ask what is excluded. Testing across devices is the item that quietly disappears first.
  • Treat a per device or per screen price the same way. We publish no such prices, because a login screen and a checkout flow with refunds are both one screen on a list and very different amounts of work.
  • Compare scope, not platforms. Two written scopes covering the same features, backend, device testing and launch support can be compared honestly. Headline numbers cannot.

3. What Drives the Android Number Up or Down

Within a band, scope decides where you land. These drivers move an Android quote most, in the order they usually move it.

The drivers that matter most:

  • Number of features in version one. The single biggest lever on any quote. Every extra flow is design, build, testing on multiple devices and maintenance, forever.
  • Backend and admin. An app that stores accounts, content or orders needs a server side, and the business usually needs an admin panel. A custom backend is the main step between the simpler band and the production grade band.
  • Payments and subscriptions. Google Play billing and account systems set up to Play policy is real work, and the work Play review examines closely.
  • Integrations. Maps, chat, third party services and data feeds each add build time and a dependency that can behave differently across devices.
  • Device range you honestly need to support. Not a surcharge, but a scope decision. Supporting the phones your actual users own is part of the job. Supporting every phone ever made is not.

Notice what is not on that list: Android itself as a line item. To lower a quote, do not negotiate the platform and do not cut the testing. Cut the feature list. Our rule for version one: one problem, one core feature set, one platform decision made honestly. Everything else goes on a named list for version two.

4. Device Fragmentation: Where Android Really Differs

This is the section that separates an honest Android quote from a hopeful one. Android runs on thousands of device models, with different screen sizes, different performance levels and different Android versions still in daily use. An app that looks perfect on a current flagship can crowd its buttons on a smaller screen, drain on an older processor, or behave differently on an older Android version.

That variety does not make Android a bad platform. For many founders it is the right first platform, because their customers are on Android. It makes the testing scope a real decision that belongs in writing before the build starts, not a surprise discovered in reviews after launch.

How a serious team handles the variety:

  • Layouts built for a range, not a phone. Screens adapt across screen sizes, then get checked on the sizes real users actually hold.
  • Performance checked on modest hardware. If the app only feels fast on the newest phone, it does not feel fast to most of the market.
  • Real devices, not only simulators. We test on real devices, because camera behaviour, notifications, payments and performance on a weak connection only show up on hardware.
  • Android versions covered by your users. The supported range follows your audience, agreed in the scope, not a guess made on launch day.

Our Android page states the principle in one line: device testing is part of the price, not an extra, because an Android app that only works on one phone is not finished. If a quote separates device testing as an optional add on, the base quote is not for a finished Android app.

5. The Small Costs Founders Forget to Budget

The build quote is not the whole first year cost. Three smaller items belong in every Android budget from the start.

Budget for these alongside the build:

  • Google Play Console: $25, one time. Google's own registration fee for the developer account your app is published under, checked on Google's Play Console sign up guidance. There is no annual renewal for the account itself. Small as it is, the account must be in your name, not your developer's. Your app, your store account, your data, from day one.
  • Maintenance: 15 to 20 percent of build cost per year. Android versions move, Play policy requirements keep moving, and dependencies age. Maintenance is the planned version of that work, instead of emergency work at the worst moment.
  • Launch fixes reserve. Every first launch finds issues no test caught, and on Android they often appear first on a device family nobody in the office owns. Hold a small reserve so a normal week one fix is a task, not a crisis. The amount is your decision; the mistake is budgeting zero.

6. The Timeline: How Long an Android App Takes, Stage by Stage

Cost is only half the question. The other half is how long an Android app takes to reach Google Play, and the answer follows the same bands as the money.

The published timeline bands:

  • Simple app: 2 to 4 months. A focused first version, a handful of screens, one main job done well.
  • Typical app: 4 to 6 months. Accounts, a backend, payments, notifications and an admin panel. This is what most founders actually build.
  • Complex app: 6 to 9 months or more. Marketplaces, social features, real time data, or anything handling a lot of users doing a lot of things at once.

Our timeline guide breaks a typical build into the phases behind those bands: discovery and scoping first, then design ending in a clickable prototype you approve, then development, usually about half the total timeline, run in sprints with a working version on your own phone. Testing overlaps the build, which matters more on Android than anywhere else, because device issues found in week six are fixes, and device issues found in launch week are delays.

What actually slows an Android project down:

  • Scope creep during the build. Features added mid build move the finish line every time, and every added feature multiplies the device testing with it.
  • Slow decisions. A question asked on Monday and answered on Friday, repeated across a project, costs more calendar time than any coding problem.
  • Design changes after development starts. Changing a screen in a design file takes minutes. In built code, across a device range, it takes days.
  • Testing left until the end. The one Android specific delay that is fully preventable. Continuous testing on real devices keeps the launch stage short.

The fastest way to ship is to cut scope, not quality and not testing. A small, excellent app that works on the phones your customers own, in three months, beats a big, fragile one in nine.

7. The Google Play Process: The Part of the Timeline You Do Not Control

Every Android timeline ends with a stage your team does not fully control. Google Play reviews apps for policy compliance, and the listing needs its data safety declarations, content details and store assets prepared properly. A team that has handled submission across hundreds of launches cannot make Play faster. It can submit an app with nothing obvious to send back, handle review questions quickly, and use a staged rollout so a problem reaches a small share of users first instead of everyone at once.

The one Play rule founders misread most:

  • Closed testing applies only to some accounts. Google requires a closed test with at least 12 testers opted in continuously for 14 days before applying for production access, but only for personal Play accounts created after 13 November 2023. Organization accounts are not subject to that requirement.
  • Check your account type before you plan around it. If you publish under a business account, which is how most of our clients publish, that step is not part of your launch timeline. If you hold a newer personal account, plan the closed test as a real stage, with tester opt ins starting early enough that the 14 days run alongside final fixes.
  • Never add it to every timeline by default. A quote that pads every Android launch by two weeks for a rule that may not apply to your account is not being careful. It is being vague.

Promise customers the submission window you control, not a launch date that assumes every Play step runs instantly. A staged rollout after approval is a feature, not a delay.

Play requirements also keep moving after launch, which is one reason the maintenance band in section 5 exists.

8. What Happens to the Cost If iPhone Follows

Many founders who start on Android plan to add iPhone later, and the right time to think about that cost is before the first line of code, because the build route you choose now decides it.

There are two honest routes. Built natively for Android only, the iPhone version later is a second build, priced as a new app layer on the shared backend. Built from the start from one cross-platform codebase, the way most of our clients launch, one codebase costs less to build and to maintain than two separate native builds. And a second platform added later almost always costs more than building it in from the start.

So the decision is really this:

  • Android only, genuinely. Your early users are on Android, budget is tight, and there is no dated plan for iPhone. Build Android well, with the device coverage in section 9 agreed in writing.
  • Both audiences at launch. Your customers are split across platforms. Price the one codebase route now, before a native build makes the second platform a second project.
  • Android now, iPhone soon and dated. Get both routes priced in writing and compare totals across the whole plan, not just the first build.

None of this changes the Android bands in section 1. It changes what the total programme costs, and it is cheaper to decide while it is still a pricing question rather than a rebuild question.

9. The Android Device Coverage and First Year Planner

Most cost articles stop at the build quote, and most Android quotes never state the testing scope at all. Use this planner instead. It has two parts: a coverage matrix you agree in writing before the build, and the first year maths for each band.

Part one, the Device Coverage Matrix. Agree these four rows in your scope:

  1. Device tiers. Current flagship, recent mid range, and the older or lower cost phones your named users actually own. Named users, from your waitlist or customers, decide the third tier, not a guess about the whole market.
  2. Screen sizes. The smallest and largest sizes in that user group, plus the common size between them. Layout problems live at the extremes, so the extremes get tested first.
  3. Android versions. The oldest version a real share of your users still runs, through to the current version. That range, written down, is what supported means for your app.
  4. Core flows on every tier. Sign up, login, the one main job of the app, and payment if the app takes money. A flow that only works on the flagship tier is not finished, whatever the demo showed.

The matrix invents no per device price, because we publish none and testing is included in the build. It turns testing from a promise into a checklist both sides can point at when a customer reports a problem on a phone nobody tested.

Part two, the first year maths:

Worked example one, validation MVP:

  • Build: $10,000 to $20,000. One core job, tested with real users, with the coverage matrix applied to the core flows.
  • Google Play Console fee from section 5, counted once, in your own account name.
  • First year maintenance at 15 to 20 percent: $1,500 to $4,000.
  • First year planning total: about $11,525 to $24,025, on a simple band timeline of 2 to 4 months.

Worked example two, simpler single platform app:

  • Build: from $25,000. A few screens, basic accounts, no custom backend, plus the Play Console fee from section 5, counted once.
  • First year maintenance at 15 to 20 percent of a $25,000 build: $3,750 to $5,000.
  • First year planning total: about $28,775 to $30,025 at the starting build price, rising with the actual build figure, on a timeline of 2 to 6 months depending on scope.

Worked example three, production grade app:

  • Build: $60,000 to $120,000 or more. Custom backend, payments, admin panel, device testing across the agreed matrix and the Play launch handled, plus the Play Console fee from section 5, counted once.
  • First year maintenance at 15 to 20 percent: $9,000 to $24,000 or more.
  • First year planning total: about $69,025 to $144,025 or more, on a timeline of 4 to 9 months or more.

The planner does not give you a quote. It gives you a defensible budget range, a launch window, and a testing scope you can hold any team to.

10. How to Get an Exact Android Number

A range becomes a number through scope, and only through scope. Any exact figure offered before a team understands your app, and the devices your users own, is theatre.

The route from range to written quote:

  • Fit check first. A short conversation about the product, the users and what version one really needs. If a team is not the right fit, a good one says so here.
  • Scope in writing, coverage included. Features listed and agreed, the things left for later named as clearly as the things included, and the device coverage matrix from section 9 written into the scope.
  • Both platform options priced. If iPhone is anywhere in your future, the quote should price the Android only route and the one codebase route side by side, so the follow on cost in section 8 is a number, not a surprise.
  • Cost and timeline together. A quote with a price and no timeline, or a timeline with no Play process allowance, is half a quote. You need both in writing before money moves.

Hold every agency you talk to, including us, to that standard. With Android, the question was never whether the platform costs more or less. It is whether the testing, the backend and the launch are honestly inside the number you were given.
