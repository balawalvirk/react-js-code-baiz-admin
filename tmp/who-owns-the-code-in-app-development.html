1. Who Owns the Code in App Development, and Why Payment Is Not Proof

Founders usually assume ownership follows payment. You paid for the build, so the app is yours. That assumption feels fair, and while the relationship is working nobody needs to test it. The gap appears later, at exactly the wrong moment: when you want to change teams, sell the business, or fix an urgent problem and the person who holds the accounts is slow, gone, or in a dispute with you.

Practical ownership is simpler and colder than the assumption. For each part of your app, ask three questions.

  • Whose name is it in? The account, the contract, or the registration names a person or a company. If that name is not you or your company, you are relying on someone else goodwill.
  • Who holds admin access? Being copied on emails is not access. Admin means you can sign in yourself, add and remove people, and recover the account if a password is lost.
  • Would it survive an exit? If the developer stopped replying today, could you still ship a fix, read your data, and pay the bills that keep the app running. If not, the control was borrowed, not owned.

Ownership is not a feeling from a friendly working relationship. It is the set of names, admin rights and recovery routes that still work on a bad day.

This post is about rights and control, not handover mechanics. If you are already mid handover, our app handover checklist covers the practical steps. Here we stay on the prior question: is it yours at all.

2. Group One: The Code Repository, in an Account You Own

The source code is the app in its editable form. Without it, no team can fix, improve or rebuild what your users have. The published checklist on our rescue page puts it first for a reason: full access to the source code and its history, in an account you own.

Check these points:

  • The repository sits in an organisation or account under your name, not under the developer personal account or agency account.
  • You are an owner or admin of that account, not a guest with viewing rights on one project.
  • The history is there. A full history shows how the code evolved. A single fresh copy with no history is weaker and harder to work from.
  • You can answer the plain question, do I own my app source code, with a screen you can open, not with a promise you were once given.

A zip file sent once by email is not the same thing. It goes stale the day the next change ships, and it proves nothing about who controls the living code. If the repository is in the developer name today, that does not automatically mean bad intent. Many small teams set accounts up quickly at the start. It does mean the fix is overdue: move it into your account while the relationship is still friendly.

3. Group Two: Store Accounts, in Your Name, With Admin Access for You

Your Apple Developer and Google Play accounts are the only doors through which your app reaches users. Whoever controls those accounts controls whether updates ship, whether listings change, and in the worst case whether the app stays live at all.

Check these points:

  • Both store accounts are registered in your name or your company name, with your email as the account holder, not the developer email.
  • You hold admin access yourself and can sign in without asking anyone to approve or relay anything.
  • The developer team has the access it needs to work, granted from your account, so it can be reviewed or removed by you.
  • Billing and renewal for the accounts run through you, so a missed payment by someone else cannot quietly put a listing at risk.

This is the group founders are most surprised by. The app carries their brand, their reviews and their revenue, yet the store account underneath carries someone else name. Putting this right early is a normal request, and a good team will expect it. 4. Group Three: Backend and Data, With the Credentials That Run Them

Most apps are two products: the part users see, and the backend that stores accounts, content, orders and history. Founders who secure the visible app and miss the backend have secured the smaller half.

Check these points:

  • You know where the backend runs and in whose account, in plain language you could repeat to a new team.
  • The database and its backups sit in that same ownership picture, not in a personal account you have never seen.
  • The credentials that run the system are recorded and reachable by you, stored properly, not held only in one person messages or memory.
  • Your user data is included. If you cannot reach the data, you cannot migrate, audit or protect the people who trusted your app.

Any move to secure backend access should protect live user data first and change nothing in production until the picture is clear. If access here is already lost, treat it as urgent. Our guide for founders whose developer has disappeared sets out the order to work in when replies have already stopped.

5. Group Four: Keys and Third Party Accounts

An app runs on outside services, grouped on the rescue checklist as API keys, payment accounts, analytics, push notifications and domains. Together they decide whether the app can charge money, send messages, be found, and be measured.

Check these points:

  • Payment accounts are in your name, so the money your app earns settles to your business and the account survives a team change.
  • The domain is registered to you or your company, with renewal and admin access under your control.
  • Analytics and push notification accounts sit under your ownership, so your user history and your ability to reach users stay with the app, not with a departing team.
  • API keys belong to accounts you own. Keys created under a developer account can be switched off from that side, and the app breaks without anyone touching its code.

Founders often discover this group last, because nothing hurts while everything is switched on. Think of these accounts as the utilities of a house. You would not let the electricity account for your own home sit in a builder name a year after the work finished.

6. Group Five: Designs and Documents

The final group is the quietest: design files, the credentials list, and anything the old team documented. It decides how expensive every future change will be.

Check these points:

  • Design files are shared into an account or workspace you control, in editable form, not only as exported images.
  • A credentials list exists and you hold it. Which services exist, which accounts run them, and who is admin on each, written down in one place you can reach.
  • Documentation the team produced is yours to keep, including setup notes and decisions recorded during the build.
  • You could brief a new team from what you hold, without the old team in the room translating their own work.

This group is also the cheapest to fix. Asking while a project is active is a short conversation. Reconstructing the files a year later is a project of its own.

7. The Ownership Proof Scorecard: Score Each Group in Ten Minutes

Here is a scorecard you can run yourself without any technical skill. For each of the five groups, answer the three control questions from section 1 and give the group one colour.

  1. Green, yours in practice. The account is in your name, you hold admin access, and your access would survive the developer leaving. No action needed beyond keeping your credentials list current.
  2. Amber, yours on paper, borrowed in practice. You believe it is yours, but the name, the admin rights or the recovery route runs through the developer. One friendly request fixes it now. The same fix gets harder every month you wait.
  3. Red, not yours in practice. It sits in someone else name, you cannot sign in yourself, or you do not know where it lives. This group goes to the top of your list, before any new feature work.

How to read your result:

  • Five greens. You own your app in the way that matters. Recheck once a year or at any team change.
  • Any ambers, no reds. The normal position, and the fix is administrative. Send the requests while the relationship is good and confirm each move in writing.
  • One red or more. Stop adding features on top of access you do not control. Secure that group first.
  • A red on store accounts or backend and data. Urgent even if the app runs fine today. Those groups control whether the app stays live and your data stays reachable.

Notice what this scorecard does not ask. It does not ask you to judge code quality or understand the technology. Rights and control can be checked by the founder personally, and that is exactly why they should be.

8. Contract and IP: Questions to Check, Not Conclusions to Assume

Founders often ask us a direct legal question about app ip ownership: my developer owns my code, is that legal, or the reverse, I paid, so the law must be on my side. We are engineers, not lawyers, and this post will not give you a legal conclusion. The wording that matters sits in your own agreement, and agreements differ. What we can give you is the questions worth checking in it, and raising with your own lawyer where the answer matters.

Questions to check in your agreement:

  • What does it say about the finished app and its source code? Look for any wording about who the work belongs to when it is delivered and paid for.
  • What does it say about code that existed before your project? Many apps include libraries or components a team already had. Check how your agreement treats that material and your right to keep using it inside your app.
  • What does it say about designs, content and data? Your brand material, your content and your user data are separate from code. Check each one is addressed.
  • What happens at the end of the relationship? Look for wording about handover of accounts, files and credentials, and any conditions attached to it.
  • What does it say about work you paid for but have not received? Unfinished work and part paid milestones are where vague agreements hurt most.
  • Is there anything you cannot find at all? Silence is also an answer. If ownership is never mentioned, that gap itself is worth raising with a lawyer before it is tested.

Ask these questions calmly and early. A professional team will not be offended by a founder reading their own contract. If the answers are unclear, a short conversation with your own lawyer is money well spent compared with a dispute later.

9. What to Do When a Group Fails the Check

A failed group is usually fixable, and the tone matters. Most ownership gaps are setup shortcuts, not traps. Work in this order.

  1. Ask in writing, while things are friendly. Name the specific account and the specific change: the repository moved to your organisation, admin added for your email on the store account. Specific requests get done. General worries get discussed.
  2. Confirm each move after it happens. Sign in yourself, check your name and your admin level, and update your credentials list the same day.
  3. Keep production safe while you work. Do not change passwords or remove access in anger mid project. Secure your position first, then adjust who else has access, with live user data protected throughout.
  4. If access is refused or replies have stopped, stop and get help before anything is changed or rebuilt. That is the disappeared developer case, and the order of recovery matters more than speed.
  5. If the picture is too tangled to read, get an outside check. Our free app check includes this ownership checklist, and the written verdict tells you what you control, what you do not, and what securing it would involve, before any paid work is discussed.

10. Mistakes That Leave Founders Owning Less Than They Think

  • Accepting guest access as ownership. A login that lets you look is not admin that lets you control, recover or remove.
  • Letting convenience decide the account name. Accounts created in a developer name at kickoff stay that way for years unless someone moves them.
  • Waiting for a dispute to check. Every fix here is easy inside a good relationship and hard inside a bad one. Timing is the whole strategy.
  • Confusing the app with the business. Your brand, reviews and users feel like the asset. The accounts underneath decide whether you keep them.
