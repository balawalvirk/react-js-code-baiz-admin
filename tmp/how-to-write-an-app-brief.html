One clear document, written before you hire anyone, turns an idea in your head into something a team can price honestly and build without constant translation.

1. What an App Brief Is, and What It Is Not

An app brief, sometimes called an app requirements document, is a plain document that describes the product you want built, in enough detail that a developer who has never met you can understand it, question it, and price it. It is written by the founder, not the developer. If the developer writes the only description of your product, you are buying their version of your idea.

A brief is not a technical specification. You do not need to name databases, frameworks or architecture, and you should not try. Choosing the stack is the developer job. A brief is also not a pitch deck. Investors need a story about a market. Developers need a description of a product: screens, flows, rules and limits.

A brief does not need to be long or technical. It needs to be specific enough that two good developers, reading it separately, would describe the same app back to you.

When a section is vague, the developer fills the gap with an assumption, and assumptions are where budgets go to die.

2. Start With the Problem and the One User

Every buildable brief opens with the problem, in the words of the person who has it. Not the feature list, not the business model, the problem. Who struggles, with what, how often, and what do they do about it today.

Then name the one user version one is for. Our rule for early products is simple: one problem, one feature, one platform to start. A brief that tries to serve customers, providers, admins and the general public in version one is really several briefs stacked together, and it will be priced like several products, because it is several products.

Write this section like this:

  • The problem in one sentence. If you cannot say it in one sentence, the product is not clear yet.
  • The primary user in one sentence. Their job or moment of need, not a demographic list.
  • What they do today instead. A spreadsheet, a phone call, a competitor, a paper form. This tells the developer what the app has to beat.

If you have not yet tested whether real people want this, say so and test first. Our guide to how to validate your app idea shows how to get real signal before development money moves. Honest briefs get respected. Inflated certainty gets priced in quietly as risk.

3. Write the Core Flows Step by Step

Features are nouns. Flows are verbs, and apps are built out of verbs. The most useful thing a founder can put in a brief is the core journey written as steps: the user opens the app, does this, sees this, taps this, and this happens. A flow written this way exposes decisions a feature list hides. Does the user need an account before they see value, or after. What happens when a payment fails. Who approves what, and where do they see it.

You do not need every flow. You need the core one, plus the supporting flows version one cannot ship without, such as sign up, payment, or an admin approving something.

For each flow, write:

  • The starting point. Where the user is and what they are trying to get done.
  • The steps in order. One action per step, including what the app shows back.
  • The awkward moments. What happens if they abandon halfway or enter the wrong thing.
  • The end state. How the user knows it worked. A confirmation, a booking, a receipt.

If a flow cannot be written as steps, it is not a feature yet. It is a wish. Wishes are fine in a notebook. They are expensive in a quote.

This section needs no design skills. It needs the clarity you would use explaining the product to a new hire on their first day, because that is exactly who is reading it.

4. Sort Features Into Must Have, Later and Out

This section decides your price more than any other. Every feature in version one adds design, build, testing and maintenance. A brief that lists thirty features with no priority tells the developer everything matters equally, which means the important things get the same attention as the decorative ones.

Sort every feature into three lists.

  • Must have. Version one fails without it. For most products this list is short, and it should be. If the app cannot do its one core job without a feature, it belongs here.
  • Later. Real, wanted, and deliberately parked. Naming these protects version one and tells the developer the direction of travel, so early choices do not block them.
  • Out. Features you have decided against. Writing out on purpose stops a quote quietly including them.

Our MVP app development guide goes deeper on what belongs in a first version. The brief version is this: a feature earns a must have place by serving the core flow from section 3. If it does not touch that flow, it is later, however much you like it.

One more habit helps. Next to any feature that sounds simple, write what it actually involves if you know. Bookings sounds simple until you add cancellations, refunds, reminders and no shows. Catching the big ones in the brief is what separates a quote that holds from a quote that grows.

5. Show Examples Instead of Describing Taste

Founders often write that they want the app to feel modern, clean or premium. Every developer nods, and every developer pictures something different. Taste cannot be transferred with adjectives. It transfers with examples.

Include examples like this:

  • Two or three apps you like, each with one sentence on what exactly you like. The booking flow, not the whole app.
  • One or two apps you do not like, with the same precision. What felt slow, cluttered or untrustworthy.
  • Anything you already have. A logo, sketches, a prototype, even photos of a whiteboard. Rough material that shows the product beats polished material that hides it.

Examples also protect you later. When a design arrives and something feels wrong, you can point back to the brief instead of arguing from memory about what was agreed.

6. Cover Platform, Services and the Practical Facts

A short practical section saves a long email chain. State which platforms version one needs, iPhone, Android or both, and say plainly if you want a recommendation. State the services the app must connect to, such as payments, maps, messaging, or a tool your business already runs on. If the app handles money, health information, children or anything regulated, say so early and clearly. Those facts change how a responsible team builds.

Add the practical facts only you know: existing accounts, an old app, a customer database, a fixed launch date. Keep ownership to one line here: name that the code and accounts will sit in your name, and leave the full ownership checklist for a separate conversation.

Do not dictate technology in this section. Need payments is a requirement. A named payment library is a guess. Requirements belong in a brief. Guesses belong in the developer proposal, where they can be explained and challenged.

7. Be Honest About Budget, in Real Ranges

The budget section is where briefs most often fail, usually by saying nothing. Founders hide the budget hoping a developer will reveal a lower price. What happens is worse. The developer guesses at your range, scopes to the guess, and the mismatch surfaces weeks in, when changing direction is expensive. A stated budget lets a good developer tell you what fits, what to cut, or that the honest answer is not to start yet.

Use real ranges. The figures we publish, checked against our live service pages today, are these. An MVP sits at $10,000 to $20,000. A simple app starts from $25,000. A production grade app runs $60,000 to $120,000 or more. A marketplace runs $80,000 and up, because it is two user apps plus an admin system. After launch, plan on maintenance at 15 to 20 percent of the build cost per year. Our app development cost page breaks those ranges down.

In the brief, state budget like this:

  • The range you can actually spend on version one, not the number you hope it costs.
  • What the range must include. Build only, or build plus launch and early maintenance.
  • What you would cut first if the range does not fit, taken from your later list in section 4.

A developer who cannot work to a stated budget should say so in the first conversation. A brief with an honest budget forces that conversation to happen while it is still free.

8. Define Success and Name Your Constraints

The last content section answers a question most briefs never ask: how will you know this worked. Keep the criteria tied to the core flow. A first user completing the core journey without help. A booking made end to end without a phone call. A task that took twenty minutes on paper now taking two in the app. You need a small number of outcomes that would make you say the money was well spent, written before the build, not invented after it.

Then name your constraints plainly. A fixed launch date and what drives it. A budget ceiling that cannot move. An existing system the app must work with. Your own availability for feedback, stated honestly, because a typical first version takes a few months from signed scope to store launch, and slow feedback is one of the few delays entirely in founder hands. Constraints named in the brief get planned around. Constraints discovered mid build become change requests.

9. The Brief Builder Template: The Whole Brief in Nine Sections

Copy these nine sections into a document, fill each one in using sections 2 to 8 above, and you have a complete app development brief template. No download, no form. The template is right here.

  1. Problem and user. The problem in one sentence, the primary user in one sentence, what they do today instead, and why now.
  2. The core flow. The main journey as numbered steps, from opening the app to knowing it worked.
  3. Supporting flows. Sign up, payment, admin or approval flows version one cannot ship without.
  4. Features, sorted. Must have, later and out. Every must have must serve the core flow.
  5. Examples and materials. Apps you like and dislike with one precise sentence each, plus sketches, brand, content or data you already have.
  6. Platform and services. iPhone, Android or both, services it must connect to, and anything regulated it touches.
  7. Practical facts. Existing accounts, old apps, customer data, fixed dates, and one line confirming code and accounts sit in your name.
  8. Budget. Your real range, what it must include, and what you would cut first.
  9. Success and constraints. The outcomes that define success, plus every hard constraint.

The Quote Ready Test: Run This Before You Send It

Before the brief goes to anyone, read it once as if you were the developer, and check these five points.

  1. Could I describe the app back? If the product is not clear on a fresh read, it will not be clear to a stranger.
  2. Could I price it without guessing? Every guess becomes either a padded price or a later argument.
  3. Is version one small enough? If nothing was cut, nothing was prioritised.
  4. Is the budget written down? Without one, quotes get written to five different imagined budgets.
  5. Would I know if it worked? If success is not defined, end of project disagreements become taste arguments, and those are unwinnable.

If the brief passes all five, send it. An hour on the brief is the cheapest hour in the whole project.

10. Mistakes That Make a Brief Impossible to Build From

Most bad briefs fail in the same few ways, and all of them are fixable before sending.

  • The feature dump. Thirty features, no flows, no priorities. The developer prices everything and prioritises nothing.
  • The clone sentence. Like Uber, but for my industry, as the whole brief. A comparison is a starting point, not a description. Your users, rules and flows are where your product lives.
  • Hidden budget, hidden constraints. Both surface eventually. In a brief they cost nothing. Mid build they cost weeks and goodwill.
  • Technology dictated by guesswork. Naming a stack you do not run removes the developer ability to recommend the right tool, while keeping the risk with you.
  • No out list. What a product refuses to do is part of its design. Without an out list, scope grows by assumption.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I tell an app developer before they quote? The problem and the one user, the core flow in steps, features sorted into must have, later and out, examples of what you like, the platforms and services involved, your real budget range, and how you will judge success.

How do I write an app brief if I am not technical? In plain words, as you would explain the product to a new hire. Flows as steps, features as lists, examples instead of adjectives. Technical choices are the developer job. Your job is clarity about the product.

Should an app brief include a budget? Yes. A stated range lets a developer tell you what fits, what to cut, or not to start. Published ranges to anchor it: an MVP at $10,000 to $20,000, a simple app from $25,000, a production grade app at $60,000 to $120,000 or more, and a marketplace at $80,000 and up.

What is the difference between an app brief and a specification? A brief describes the product, user, flows and limits, written by the founder before hiring. A specification describes the technical build in detail, and is normally produced with the developer after the brief.

Can I use this as an app development brief template? Yes. Section 9 is the template. Copy the nine sections into your own document and fill them in. There is nothing to download, the template lives in this post on purpose.

What happens after I send a good brief? A good team turns it into a written plan with features, cost and timeline before build work starts, and questions the weak spots. If a team quotes instantly with no questions, that tells you something about the quote.
