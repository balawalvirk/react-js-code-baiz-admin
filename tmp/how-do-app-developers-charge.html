A founder gets three quotes for the same app. One is a single fixed number. One is a low hourly rate with no total attached. One is a monthly amount with no end date. All three look cheaper than the other two, depending on which line you read first. That confusion is normal, because how do app developers charge is really three different ways of deciding who carries the risk when the work changes, and the work always changes.

TL;DR: Fixed price suits a tightly defined first build, because the total is written before work starts and the agency carries the cost of overruns inside that scope. Hourly suits small, uncertain jobs, but it has no ceiling, so the founder carries the risk. An app developer retainer suits a live product that ships improvements every month, because the team, the knowledge and the response stay in place. Most founders do best starting fixed for version one, then moving to a retainer after launch. Run the fit test in section 8 before you sign anything.

1. The Three App Development Pricing Models, At a Glance

Every quote you receive will be built on one of these three models, or a mix of two of them. Our app development cost page publishes the build ranges these models are applied to.

  • Fixed price: one written total for a defined scope. A simple app starts from $25,000, production grade work runs $60,000 to $120,000 and above, and a marketplace runs $80,000 and up. The model decides how that total is charged, not whether it exists.
  • Hourly: you pay for time spent, at an agreed rate. US agency teams commonly sit in an industry range of about $75 to $150 per hour, framed here as an industry range, not as any one agency rate. There is no total until the work ends.
  • Retainer: you pay a monthly amount for an ongoing team. The amount buys continued capacity, product knowledge and a standing team. No honest retainer figure can be compared without knowing what work and what team it actually covers.
  • Maintenance is its own line, whichever model you choose. Plan on 15 to 20 percent of the original build cost per year for updates, monitoring, store changes and small fixes. A tightly scoped MVP first version can run $10,000 to $20,000, and it still needs that maintenance line after launch.

The model is not the price. The model is the answer to one question: when the plan changes, who pays? Read every quote as an answer to that question first, and as a number second.

2. Fixed Price: What You Are Actually Buying

Fixed price means the scope is written down, feature by feature, and a total is agreed for that scope before the build begins. Changes are priced in writing before they are done, not added afterwards as a surprise.

What fixed price does well:

  • Budget certainty. You know the total for version one before the real money moves, which matters when the money is set aside in advance.
  • Risk sits with the agency, inside the scope. If a listed feature takes longer than expected, that overrun is the agency problem, not a longer invoice for you.

3. Where Fixed Price Burns Founders

Fixed price fails in predictable places, and none of them are the model itself. They are what happens around it.

  • The scope was never real. A founder signs a fixed price for an app described in two paragraphs. Halfway through, both sides discover they meant different products. Every discovery becomes a paid change, and the fixed total quietly stops meaning anything.
  • The cheapest fixed quote wins. A low fixed number usually means something was removed quietly, often testing, backend work or store launch help. The app arrives, and the missing work arrives with it, priced as a second project.
  • Everything is treated as a change. A fair agency prices genuine new features as changes. A difficult one treats normal clarification, small copy changes and bug fixes as changes too. Ask early how fixes inside the agreed scope are handled. The answer tells you a lot.

Check any fixed quote for a named scope list, named exclusions, and changes priced in writing before work. Our mobile app development page sets out that sequence. Fixed price is the right start for most first builds, but only when the scope is detailed enough that two strangers would build the same app from it.

4. Hourly: What You Are Actually Buying

Hourly means you pay for the time a team spends, reported as hours against tasks. It is the simplest model to start and the hardest model to predict.

What hourly does well:

  • Flexibility. When the job itself is genuinely unclear, for example a short investigation, a small fix list or early technical exploration, hourly lets work start without pretending the scope is known.
  • No scope document needed to begin. That is also its weakness, but for a two week diagnostic job it can be the honest choice.
  • You only pay for time used. On a genuinely small job, that can cost less than any fixed quote that has to cover risk.

5. Where Hourly Burns Founders

  • There is no ceiling. An hourly job ends when the work ends or the money runs out. Founders regularly discover that the second ending arrives first.

On hourly work, check who is billed, how time is reported against named tasks, and what happens when the estimate doubles. A lower rate is not a lower total if the team is slower. Hourly has a place, in short, bounded, uncertain work. As the main model for building a whole first version, it asks a non-technical founder to carry the exact risk they hired an agency to remove. 6. A Retainer: What You Are Actually Buying

A retainer means an ongoing monthly arrangement with a team that knows your product. The work is not one fixed build. It is a steady flow of improvements, fixes, updates and support, planned month by month.

What a retainer does well:

  • Continuity. The same engineers who built the app keep working on it. Their knowledge of your codebase, your users and your past decisions does not walk away at launch.
  • A living product rhythm. Successful apps ship improvements every month. A retainer matches how a live product actually runs, instead of forcing every improvement into a new project and a new quote.
  • Priorities can move. When user evidence changes the plan, the monthly work changes with it, without a change request battle over every adjustment.

7. Where a Retainer Burns Founders

  • Paying for capacity you do not use. A retainer signed before a product has live users, real feedback or a monthly improvement list is often money spent standing still. Retainers earn their keep after launch, not before there is anything to improve.
  • No monthly evidence. If a month passes with no shipped version, no summary and no clear next priorities, you are not on a product retainer. You are on a subscription to status meetings.

Check any retainer for what work it covers, how the month is planned and reported, and how it changes or ends. Maintenance at 15 to 20 percent per year may sit inside it, so confirm you are not paying for the same cover twice.

A simple stage rule covers most cases: fixed price to get a defined version one built, hourly only for short uncertain jobs, and a retainer once the product is live and changing every month.

8. Run the Pricing Model Fit Test

Answer six questions honestly. Each answer points to one model. Count the points at the end.

  1. How certain is the scope? If you can list version one feature by feature, score fixed price. If the job is an investigation with no clear end, score hourly. If the list changes every month based on users, score retainer.
  2. How often will priorities change? Rarely before launch scores fixed price. Constantly, in a live product, scores retainer. For a one off job where change is the whole point, score hourly.
  3. Where is the product today? Not built yet scores fixed price. Live, with paying users and a backlog, scores retainer. Built, but with a small named problem to diagnose, scores hourly.
  4. How much product time do you have? If you cannot manage a team week to week, score fixed price or retainer, and count this question for the one you already lead on. If you can review hours and direct tasks yourself, score hourly.
  5. What budget shape do you need? A total you must not exceed scores fixed price. A steady monthly amount you can plan around scores retainer. A small open amount for a short job scores hourly.
  6. What happens after launch? If launch is the finish line for now, score fixed price. If launch is the starting line and improvements are already queued, score retainer. If there is no launch yet, only a question to answer, score hourly.

How to read your score:

  • Four or more for one model: that model fits your stage. Sign on that basis, and use the checks in sections 2, 4 or 6 to test the actual quote.
  • A split score, three and three: you probably need a sequence, not a compromise. The common sequence is fixed price for version one, then a retainer after launch. That sequence is written into the cost page guidance because it is how most of our clients actually run.
  • Hourly leading on a full build: pause. Hourly leading usually means the scope is not ready to price yet. Shrink the job to a defined first version first. Our MVP app development guide shows how to cut a first version down to one core job that can be fixed in scope.

9. Compare Quotes on the Same Scope, Not on the Headline

Whichever model you lean toward, force every quote onto the same scope before you compare numbers. Use this short worksheet.

  1. Write the scope once. One list of features, screens, integrations and exclusions, and give the identical list to every team you ask.
  2. Ask each team for its model and its total shape. Fixed teams give a total. Hourly teams give a rate, an hour estimate and a reporting method. Retainer teams give a monthly amount and a clear list of what that month covers.
  3. Ask what is not included. Testing, backend, store submission, design revisions, maintenance and aftercare. Write the answers next to each quote, because the gaps are where the real price difference usually sits.
  4. Ask how change is handled. Priced in writing before work, billed by the hour as it happens, or absorbed into next month priorities. There is no wrong answer here, only an answer you should hear before you sign, not after.
  5. Project the first year, not the first invoice. Add the build, plus maintenance at 15 to 20 percent of build cost for the year, plus the likely first round of post launch improvements in the model each team proposed. The cheapest first invoice is often not the cheapest first year.

10. Which Model Fits Which Stage

  • Idea stage, no product yet: fixed price for a scoped first version. If the idea itself is still unproven, keep that first version tight. An MVP at $10,000 to $20,000, built to one core job, is a fixed scope job.
  • A small, unclear technical job: hourly, tightly bounded. A diagnosis, a short fix list or an exploration can be hourly, as long as you review reported time often and agree in advance when you will stop and reassess.
  • Live product with users: retainer. Once improvements, store changes and user feedback arrive every month, a retainer with the team that knows the product beats restarting a hiring and quoting cycle for every change.
  • Marketplace or two sided product: fixed to launch, retainer to grow. Marketplaces at $80,000 and up carry payments, trust and safety work that keeps evolving after launch, so the build and the running model are usually different.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is fixed price or hourly better for a first app? For a first app, fixed price is usually safer. The scope can be listed, the total is written before work starts, and the agency carries overrun risk inside that scope. Hourly suits a short, unclear job. As the main model for a whole first version, it leaves the total open, and the founder carries that risk.

When does an app developer retainer make sense? Once the app is live and changing every month. A retainer keeps the same team, the same codebase knowledge and a steady improvement rhythm in place. Before launch, with no users and no monthly change list, a retainer is usually paying for capacity you cannot use yet. Many founders start fixed for version one and move to a retainer after launch.

Why do hourly quotes look cheaper at the start? Because a rate is not a total. An hourly quote shows you the smaller number and leaves the larger one, the final hour count, unknown. Compare an hourly quote by its estimated hours, its reporting method and what happens when the estimate doubles, not by the rate alone. US agency teams commonly sit at about $75 to $150 per hour as an industry range.

What should maintenance cost, whichever model I choose? Plan on 15 to 20 percent of the original build cost per year. That covers operating system and store updates, monitoring and small fixes. Under a fixed build it is a separate line after launch. Under a retainer it may be included, so check in writing that you are not paying for the same cover twice.

Can I change models later? Yes, and most long running products do. The natural path is fixed price for a defined version one, hourly only for small uncertain jobs along the way, and a retainer once the product is live and improving monthly. What matters is that each stage is agreed in writing, with its model named, before that stage starts.
