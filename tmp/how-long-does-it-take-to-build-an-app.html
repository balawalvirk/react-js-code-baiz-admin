TL;DR: How long does it take to build an app? A simple app takes 2 to 4 months. A typical app with accounts, payments, and real users takes 4 to 6 months. A complex product takes 6 to 9 months or more. The biggest timeline killer is not the coding, it is slow decisions and unclear scope.

The number one question founders ask me, right after cost, is how long it will take. And the honest answer is: it depends, but not in the vague way people say it depends. After more than 300 projects since 2019, we can predict timelines pretty well, because apps break down into the same phases every time.

Here are the headline numbers, so you do not have to read the whole post to get your answer.

A simple app takes 2 to 4 months. This is a focused first version, a handful of screens, one main thing it does well.

A typical app takes 4 to 6 months. This is what most founders actually build: user accounts, a backend, payments, notifications, an admin panel. Enough to run a real business on.

A complex app takes 6 to 9 months or more. Think marketplaces, social features, real-time data, or anything that has to handle a lot of users doing a lot of things at once.

Most projects that run late do not run late because of coding. They run late because of slow decisions, changing scope, and feedback loops that take a week when they should take a day.

Now let me show you where those numbers come from, phase by phase.

Quick Answers to the Most Common Timeline Questions

How long does it take to build an app from idea to launch? For a typical first version, 4 to 6 months. That covers scoping, design, development, testing, and getting through the app store review process.

Can an app be built in one month? A very simple prototype, sometimes. A production app that real customers depend on, no. If someone promises your full app in 30 days, they are either cutting serious corners or do not understand what you are asking for.

What phase takes the longest? Development, the actual building. It is usually about half the total timeline. But the phase that causes the most delays is rarely development, it is the gaps between phases, when decisions wait for answers.

Does building for both iPhone and Android double the timeline? Not if the team builds smart. We use React Native, which means one codebase runs on both platforms. You do not build two apps, you build one app that ships to both. That typically saves weeks compared to building two separate native apps.

How long does the app store review take? Apple usually reviews in 24 to 72 hours. Google Play is similar for new apps, sometimes a bit longer for a first submission. Rejections happen, usually for small policy issues, and each round adds a few days. We always budget a week or two at the end for this.

1. Weeks 1-2: Discovery and Scoping

Every app project starts here, whether the team admits it or not. Discovery is where you and the team agree on what you are actually building, who it is for, and what version one includes versus what waits for later.

This phase produces a feature list, wireframes or sketches of the main screens, and a technical plan: what the backend looks like, which third-party services you need (payments, maps, push notifications), and how the work gets broken into build chunks.

Why it matters for your timeline: most doomed projects are doomed here. If this phase gets skipped or rushed, the team starts building something they are not sure about, and every uncertainty surfaces later as a delay, when it costs five times more to fix.

What slows it down: unclear answers about what the app should do, founders who want everything in version one, and too many decision-makers arguing over details that do not matter yet.

How long does it take to build an app if discovery is skipped? Longer, always. Skipping two weeks of planning does not save two weeks. It adds two months of rework later. The projects that ship on time are the projects that started with a clear plan.

2. Weeks 3-5: UX and UI Design

Design is where the app becomes real. UX, the user experience, is about how the app flows: what happens when you tap what, where the buttons go, how many steps it takes to do something. UI, the interface, is how it looks: colors, fonts, spacing, the overall feel.

By the end of this phase you get clickable prototypes, screens you can tap through on your own phone that look and feel like the finished app but do not do anything yet. This is the last cheap moment to change your mind. Moving a button in a design file takes minutes. Moving it after the app is built takes days.

Why it matters for your timeline: design is where founders can give the most valuable feedback with the least cost. The founders who engage here, tap the prototype, send detailed notes within a day or two, end up with apps that ship on schedule. The ones who go quiet for three weeks and then ask for a full redesign are how timelines double.

What slows it down: redesign rounds that start from scratch, conflicting feedback from five different people, and chasing a trendy look instead of settling on a clean, functional one.

3. Weeks 6-14: Development, the Actual Build

This is the longest phase, usually about half the total timeline. Developers turn the designs into a working app: the screens you tap, the logic behind them, the backend servers that store data, and all the integrations with third-party services.

Development almost always happens in weekly cycles. Each week, the team finishes a chunk of working features, and you can see real progress. That rhythm matters. If a team goes dark for six weeks and then shows you everything at once, you have no chance to catch problems early.

Why it matters for your timeline: this is where the technical decisions from discovery either pay off or come back to bite. A well-planned architecture lets developers move fast. A rushed one means the team keeps stopping to rebuild things they thought were done.

What slows it down: features getting added mid-build (the single biggest killer), slow answers to technical questions, and third-party services that do not work the way their documentation promised.

A note on platforms: with React Native, the team builds once and ships to both iPhone and Android. Bug fixes land everywhere at once, and updates go out to both stores together. Teams building two separate native apps have to do every feature, every fix, and every test twice. That is real calendar time, not just cost.

4. Weeks 10-16: Testing and QA, Overlapping the Build

Testing does not wait for development to finish. Good teams start testing as soon as the first features are done. QA engineers try to break the app the way real users will: bad internet, old phones, tapping buttons in the wrong order, doing everything backwards.

There is also a phase founders forget about: beta testing with real people. Getting the app into the hands of 10 to 20 friendly users for a week or two surfaces problems no internal tester will find, because real users are gloriously unpredictable.

Why it matters for your timeline: the projects that test early and continuously ship on time. The projects that test at the very end find all the bugs at the worst possible moment, when every fix has to be squeezed into a timeline with no slack left.

What slows it down: testing left until the end, bugs reported vaguely ("it is broken" with no details), and skipping beta testing to save two weeks, which usually costs four weeks after launch when real users find the bugs instead.

5. Weeks 15-17: Launch and Store Review

The app is built and tested. Now it goes to the app stores. Both Apple and Google review every app before it goes live. They check for policy compliance, crashes, misleading descriptions, and privacy issues.

Apple typically reviews in 24 to 72 hours. Google Play is usually similar for updates, sometimes longer for brand-new apps. Rejections are normal, especially for first submissions, and are usually about small policy details, not your whole app. Each rejection and resubmission adds a few days.

Why it matters for your timeline: this phase is the one with the most outside-your-control time. You cannot rush Apple's review queue. That is why experienced teams budget one to two weeks here and start the process with a fully polished build.

What slows it down: last-minute changes submitted to the store, privacy policy gaps, missing screenshots or descriptions, and rejection reasons that require actual code changes.

Timeline by App Complexity

Here is the whole picture in one table, using the phases above:

  • Simple MVP — Example features: 5-10 screens, one core feature, no accounts; Typical timeline: 2 to 4 months
  • Typical app — Example features: Accounts, backend, payments, notifications, admin panel; Typical timeline: 4 to 6 months
  • Complex app — Example features: Marketplace, social features, real-time data, custom backend; Typical timeline: 6 to 9 months
  • Enterprise-grade — Example features: Multiple roles, integrations, compliance, scale requirements; Typical timeline: 9 to 12+ months

The number founders should really remember: the fastest way to ship is to cut scope, not quality. A small, excellent app that launches in 3 months beats a big, mediocre app that launches in 9 months. Every time.

Note the pattern: more features do not just add linear time. Every feature adds screens, logic, tests, and edge cases. An app with twice the features often takes three times as long, because the pieces interact with each other. This is why good teams push hard to keep version one focused.

What Really Slows Projects Down

Across more than 300 projects, the same five delays show up again and again. None of them are about how fast developers type.

1. Scope creep. "Can we just add one small thing?" One small thing is never one small thing. Every feature needs design, code, testing, and maintenance. A project that keeps growing during the build will never end on time, because the finish line keeps moving.

2. Slow decisions. The team asks a question on Monday and gets an answer on Friday. Multiply that by 50 questions over a project, and you have lost months to waiting. The single best thing a founder can do for their timeline is answer quickly and decisively.

3. Redesigns during development. Changing the design after coding has started is the most expensive change you can make. This is why the clickable prototype phase exists. Decide there, not during week 10 of the build.

4. Third-party services. Payments, maps, chat, identity verification, all the services apps plug into. Sometimes they work exactly as documented. Sometimes they do not, and the team burns a week working around a service that was supposed to save time.

5. Unclear ownership. Nobody is sure who gives final approval, so every decision loops through three people. App projects need one person who can say yes or no. If that person is you, own it. If it is not you, make sure that person is in the loop from day one.

How to Keep Your Project on Schedule

You cannot control everything, but you can control the things that matter most.

  • Start with an MVP. Define version one as the smallest app that proves your idea works. Everything else goes on a list for version two.
  • Answer fast. Aim for same-day answers to team questions. A founder who responds quickly can save their project a month or more.
  • Give feedback in writing. Vague calls lead to misinterpretation. Written feedback with specific references ("on the checkout screen, move the button up") gets fixed right the first time.
  • Freeze the design before development. Sign off on the prototype, and treat changes after that as new work with new time, because that is what they are.
  • Pick one decision-maker. Every project needs one person whose yes means yes. More cooks do not make a better app, they make a slower one.
  • Plan for launch early. App store accounts, privacy policies, screenshots, descriptions. None of this is hard, but all of it takes time, and doing it in the final week is how launches slip.
