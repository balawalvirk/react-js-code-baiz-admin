Your Questions, Answered First

How much does it cost to hire a mobile app developer? For a first version of a real app, expect roughly $20,000 to $80,000 with an agency, and $5,000 to $25,000 with a freelancer. Rates vary by region and scope. The cheapest quote is rarely the cheapest project, because a bad build costs you a rebuild.

Should I hire a freelancer or an app development agency? Hire a freelancer for small, well-defined tasks with an expert to supervise the work. Hire an agency when you need a team, reliability, and accountability on a full product. Most first-time founders are better served by an agency.

How long does it take to build an app? A typical first version takes two to six months, depending on complexity. Anyone who promises a full custom app in two weeks is cutting corners you will pay for later.

How do I know if a developer is good before hiring them? You cannot judge code if you do not write code. You can judge process. Ask how they handle testing, how they hand over the code, who owns the accounts, and what happens when something breaks after launch. Vague answers are the red flag.

What is the biggest mistake founders make when hiring an app developer? Choosing on price alone. The difference between a $10,000 build and a $25,000 build often decides whether you ship once or pay twice.

1. Decide What You Are Actually Buying

Before you hire a mobile app developer, get clear on the job. There are three different hires hiding under one phrase:

The freelancer

One person, hired for a defined piece of work. Good for prototypes, single features, or ongoing maintenance when you already have a technical lead.

The agency

A team with designers, developers, and project management. Good for building a full product from idea to launch. You get process, backup capacity, and someone accountable for the outcome.

The in-house developer

A full-time employee on your payroll. Good when the app is your whole business and you need someone permanent. Expensive to hire, expensive to fire, and one person still cannot do everything.

For most first-time founders building their first app, the real choice is between a freelancer and an agency. That is where this guide focuses: the freelance app developer vs agency decision.

2. Freelancer vs Agency: An Honest Comparison

  • Cost — Freelancer: Lower upfront, $5k to $25k typical; Agency: Higher upfront, $20k to $80k typical
  • Speed — Freelancer: Fast start, one person's calendar; Agency: Slower start, more thorough process
  • Skill coverage — Freelancer: One skill set, may need others; Agency: Design, development, testing, project management
  • Accountability — Freelancer: Limited, hard to enforce; Agency: Contract, reputation, a team on the line
  • Continuity risk — Freelancer: High, if they disappear the project stalls; Agency: Low, the team continues if one person leaves
  • Best for — Freelancer: Small defined tasks, prototypes; Agency: Full product builds, founders without technical staff

There is a pattern we see constantly. A founder hires a freelancer because the quote is half the agency price. The freelancer is good, but one person cannot cover design, development, testing, and project management at a professional level. The app launches with bugs, the freelancer gets busy with other clients, fixes stop coming, and the founder starts over. The total bill is the cheap quote plus the rebuild.

Hiring the cheapest developer is not saving money. It is borrowing money from your future rebuild.

That does not mean freelancers are bad. Some are excellent. It means you need to match the hire to the job, and you need to know how to vet them.

3. The Vetting Checklist: 10 Questions Before You Sign

You do not need to be technical to hire well. You need to ask questions that reveal process. A good developer answers these directly. A bad one deflects.

1. Who owns the code, the app store accounts, and the backend? Everything must be registered in your name or your company's name. Developers who insist on keeping the accounts in their name are building leverage, not your app.

2. How do you handle testing before launch? Look for a real answer: testing on multiple devices, a test version you approve before the public launch, and a plan for fixing bugs found late.

3. What happens when something breaks after launch? Every app needs a support period. A developer who disappears the day the app goes live is not finished, they have just stopped billing you.

4. Can I see an app you built that is live on the App Store or Google Play right now? Live apps, not screenshots. Download one. Use it. If it crashes or feels broken, you have your answer.

5. Who will actually work on my project? Agencies sometimes show you a senior team and hand the work to juniors. Ask for the names and experience of the people on your project.

6. How do you communicate, and how often? You want regular updates in plain language, not silence followed by a surprise. Weekly updates with a demo you can tap through are the standard.

7. What is your process for changes mid-project? Scope changes are normal. The question is whether they are tracked, priced honestly, and approved by you before work starts.

8. How do you hand over the project at the end? You should receive the full source code, documentation, and credentials for every service. If a developer cannot describe their handover, they have never done one properly.

9. What technology will you build with, and why? You do not need to understand the answer. You need to hear a reason tied to your app, not a religion about one technology. For most apps, a cross-platform framework like React Native is the sensible choice, and we say that as a team that builds with it daily.

10. Can I talk to a past client? References are the strongest signal available to a non-technical founder. A developer with happy clients is eager to connect you. A developer with none will make excuses.

4. Red Flags That Should Stop You

Some signals are worth walking away from, no matter how good the price looks.

  1. No live apps to show. A portfolio of mockups is not a portfolio of apps.
  2. The quote is dramatically cheaper than everyone else. Half the market rate means half the work, half the testing, or both.
  3. They want full payment upfront. Standard practice is staged payments tied to milestones you can verify.
  4. Everything is in their accounts. Code in their repository, app in their developer account, backend on their server. This is how founders lose their own apps.
  5. No written contract. A handshake deal on a $30,000 build is a donation, not a contract.
  6. They cannot explain their process in plain words. If they cannot explain it to you, they cannot manage it for you.
  7. They badmouth every previous client. Today's previous client is tomorrow's you.
  8. Guaranteed timelines with no caveats. Honest developers give ranges and explain what could move them.

5. What a Bad Hire Really Costs

Founders usually compare quotes. They rarely compare the cost of failure. Here is what a bad hire actually costs, beyond the invoice:

The rebuild. You pay a second developer to rebuild what the first one broke. We see rebuilds cost 60 to 100 percent of the original budget, on top of what you already spent.

The lost months. A failed build burns three to six months. For a startup, that is the most expensive currency there is. Competitors ship while you restart.

The damaged product. Apps with one-star reviews do not recover easily. Launching broken poisons the listing, and every future download pays the price of those early reviews.

The stress. We hear this from founders constantly. The money hurts, but the months of chasing an unresponsive developer, watching deadlines slide, and doubting the whole idea hurt more.

When you hire a mobile app developer, you are not buying hours of coding. You are buying the certainty that the app gets built right the first time. Price that certainty into the decision.

6. How to Structure the Deal

Once you have found a developer you trust, structure the engagement so the incentives stay aligned.

Pay in milestones, not upfront

Break the project into phases: design approval, working prototype, beta version, launch. Pay when each phase is delivered and approved by you. Never pay the full amount before you have seen working software.

Put everything in writing

The contract should cover the scope, the timeline, the payment schedule, who owns the code and accounts, the support period after launch, and what happens if either side wants out. If the developer resists a written contract, walk away.

Keep the accounts in your name

Register the Apple Developer account, the Google Play account, the domain, and the hosting in your company's name from day one. This is non-negotiable. It is your app.

Start with a small paid test

Before committing to a full build, pay for a small defined piece of work: a design sprint, a prototype, a technical review of your idea. Two weeks and a few thousand dollars will tell you more about a developer than ten sales calls.

Never give a developer control of assets you cannot take back. Code, accounts, and credentials belong to you from day one.

7. Where to Find Good Developers

Good developers are found through trust networks, not job boards alone. For an app developer for startup builds, a referral from a founder who already shipped beats any marketplace rating.

  • Referrals from other founders. Ask founders who have shipped apps who they used and whether they would hire them again.
  • Clutch and similar review platforms. Look for agencies with detailed written reviews, not just star ratings.
  • App store credits. Find apps you admire, check who built them. Many list the agency in the app description or on their website.
  • Local meetups and founder communities. A developer you can meet in person is a developer who cannot disappear as easily.

Avoid hiring purely on freelance marketplace ratings. A five-star rating for building WordPress sites says nothing about building your app.
